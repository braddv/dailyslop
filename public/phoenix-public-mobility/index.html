A public option for getting around

What if Phoenix
owned the ride?

A region-scale network of autonomous cars, pooled vans and neighborhood buses could make mobility universal. But replacing 80% of personal driving is not a fleet purchase. It is a new public utility.

Why sharing changes the fleet math

Today’s cars are mostly parking.
A public fleet keeps moving.

Metro Phoenix households have about 3.70 million personal vehicles. Most serve one household and sit parked between trips. A robotaxi can serve many households in the same day, so replacing trips does not require replacing private cars one-for-one. In the default model, each public vehicle substitutes for roughly 10 household vehicles at the 80% target.

The commute peak still governs capacity. High all-day utilization cannot move a region whose riders all request solo cars at 8 AM and 5 PM. The network must pool commute trips into vans and buses, retain rail and high-capacity routes, reposition before demand surges and keep a reserve fleet. A real procurement model must be sized against the busiest service hour—not merely average daily mileage.

Annual operations

Actual transit performance versus a speculative autonomous model.

The useful answer is probably “both.”

A Phoenix network should use rail or true BRT for the strongest peak corridors, pooled autonomous vans as feeders and cross-town service, and small robotaxis for low-density, accessible and late-night trips. Building the entire system as individual robotaxis wastes pooling; building only fixed corridors leaves much of metropolitan Phoenix beyond convenient walking access.

Do not read the operating-cost row as a forecast victory for autonomy. FTA figures are observed 2023 national expenses at actual ridership. The public-fleet figure is an engineering assumption that excludes capital amortization, vehicle replacement, financing, land, insurance uncertainty and proprietary autonomy licensing. Comparable alternatives require the same Phoenix ridership, service hours and lifecycle accounting.

The congestion question

Autonomy does not
automatically help.

Traffic falls only when higher occupancy saves more miles than empty repositioning adds. The comparison below reports the change in all regional VMT—not merely the miles inside the replaced slice.

The binding rule

Price solo rides higher, make pooled service the default, cap empty miles, reserve curbs and integrate vans with fixed-route rail and bus. Without those rules, a convenient public robotaxi can make Phoenix traffic worse.

How it would work

One network.
Three service patterns.

The public authority sets coverage, fares, labor, accessibility and data rules. Autonomous-driving vendors compete to supply certified technology and fleet operations under auditable contracts.

01

Cars fill the gaps

Door-to-door, accessible service for low-density areas, late nights, disability trips and connections that cannot pool efficiently.

02

Vans pool corridors

Dynamic stops and short walking windows combine nearby origins and destinations. Transfers occur at shaded neighborhood mobility hubs.

03

Buses anchor demand

High-frequency autonomous shuttles stay on repeatable trunk routes. Rail and full-size fixed-route buses remain where capacity is highest.

04

The dispatcher orchestrates

A regional platform forecasts demand, assigns the smallest adequate vehicle, protects maximum waits and prevents discriminatory service gaps.

05

Depots keep it moving

Distributed charging, cleaning, maintenance and remote assistance facilities operate around the clock with heat-resilient equipment and backup power.

06

Every rider can enter

App, web, phone, cash-compatible fare media and station kiosks. ADA service is a core fleet requirement, not a separate afterthought.

A credible path

Earn the right
to scale.

The 80% case is a horizon test, not a launch plan. Expansion should stop automatically when pooling, safety, cost or service-quality gates fail.

  1. PILOT5,000 vehicles

    Airport, medical, university and late-night transit connections. Prove accessible dispatch and publish empty-mile data.

  2. NETWORK25,000 vehicles

    Build shared mobility hubs and integrate fares with Valley Metro. Require at least 35% of passenger-miles in pooled vehicles.

  3. REGIONAL75,000 vehicles

    Expand only after wait-time equity, safety, cost and congestion targets hold across a full Phoenix summer.

  4. UTILITYScale by evidence

    Move toward majority replacement only if empty mileage remains below 15% and total regional VMT is demonstrably falling.

What could break

The hard parts are
governance.

Vendor lock-in

Waymo does not currently sell an off-the-shelf public bus-and-van system. Separate vehicle ownership, dispatch data and autonomy licensing wherever technically possible.

Induced demand

Cheap, effortless trips create new travel and pull riders from walking and transit. Use fares, pooling defaults and road pricing to protect the VMT goal.

Peak concentration

A fleet sized for the morning peak sits idle later. Guarantee commute pooling and preserve high-capacity fixed routes instead of sizing every trip as a car.

Safety + liability

Require public incident reporting, independent audits, cyber standards, insurance and a clear authority to suspend unsafe software or routes.

Labor transition

Automation removes driving work but creates maintenance, cleaning, dispatch, rider-support and infrastructure roles. Bargain the transition before procurement.

Extreme heat

Air-conditioning load, battery degradation and charging reliability make Phoenix a demanding operating environment. Test through summer before each scale gate.

Read the estimate correctly

A feasibility model,
not a bid.

Vehicle prices include planning allowances for autonomous hardware and fleet integration because no public Waymo procurement price exists. The default car is $160K, van $260K and 24-seat bus $850K. Direct operating assumptions are $0.80, $1.20 and $2.40 per fleet-mile, plus 15% administration. Capital adds a 15% spare fleet and 30% for charging, depots, control systems and contingency.

The default mix carries 45% of passenger-miles in cars averaging 1.8 occupants, 35% in vans averaging 5 and 20% in buses averaging 14. Empty miles are 22%, 15% and 8%. Outcomes are linearly scaled and do not model time-of-day peaks, roadway capacity, induced demand, capital financing, land, taxes, fleet replacement or proprietary autonomy licensing.

Expected uncertainty: at least ±35% on initial capital and ±30% on annual operating cost before a Phoenix-specific request for information and microsimulation.
